8 Food Safety Myths That Can Make You Sick

November 18, 2026 · Life Tank

Washing raw chicken, the five second rule, smelling leftovers to see if they're still good. These habits feel safe, but food safety experts say they can actually raise your risk of food poisoning.

Eight kitchen habits that can make you sick. They feel safe. Most of us grew up with them. But food safety experts say they're wrong. You probably did at least one this week. And number eight is about something most Filipino homes cook every single day.

Why this matters

Food poisoning isn't always dramatic. Sometimes it's just an upset stomach. A bad night, or a missed day of work. So people often blame the wrong thing. Bacteria grow fastest in the danger zone. That's between four and sixty degrees Celsius. Room temperature in the Philippines sits right inside it. So small kitchen habits matter more than you think. Let's start with the one people argue about most.

1. Washing raw chicken

Many of us grew up washing raw chicken. It feels cleaner. But it isn't. Rinsing doesn't remove the bacteria that matter. Instead, water splashes them around your sink. Onto the counter, the dish rack, even your clothes. Cooking is what makes chicken safe. Heat kills the bacteria. Water just spreads them. What if there are bits you want off? Pat it with a paper towel, then throw it away. Then wash your hands with soap right after. Making adobo tonight? Take the chicken straight from the pack to the pot. No rinse needed. The cooking does the work.

2. The five second rule

Dropped a piece of food? Five second rule, right? Researchers at Rutgers University actually tested this. Bacteria can transfer in less than one second. Time matters a little. Moisture matters more. Wet food like watermelon picked up the most. Dry gummy candy picked up the least. The surface matters too. Tile and stainless steel transferred more than carpet. So the rule isn't really about seconds. It's about what you dropped, and where. A wet slice of fruit on kitchen tile? That's a bin, not a rescue. When in doubt, throw it out.

3. Letting hot food cool first

Here's a habit almost every home has. You cook a big pot of sinigang. Then you leave it on the stove to cool. Many believe hot food in the fridge is bad. But leaving it out is the bigger risk. As food cools, it sits in the danger zone. Guidance says refrigerate leftovers within two hours. On days above thirty two degrees, within one hour. The smarter way is to split big batches. Use small, shallow containers. They cool faster and more evenly. Leave a little space around them in the fridge. And the next myth surprises a lot of people.

4. Thawing meat on the counter

It's the frozen pork you leave out all afternoon. The outside thaws first and warms up. While the center is still frozen solid. So the surface sits in the danger zone for hours. There are three safe ways to thaw. One: in the fridge, usually overnight. Two: in cold water, inside a sealed bag. Change the water every thirty minutes. Three: in the microwave, then cook it right away. And here's a bonus most people don't know. You can cook meat straight from frozen. It just takes about half again as long. Planning tomorrow's lunch? Move the meat to the fridge before bed tonight.

5. Freezing kills bacteria

Speaking of the freezer, here's a common myth. Freezing kills bacteria. It doesn't. Freezing only puts most bacteria on pause. Once food thaws, they can start growing again. That's why thawed meat needs the same care as fresh. Thorough cooking is what destroys them. It's also why freezing old food doesn't rescue it. Freeze food while it's still fresh. Not when it's about to turn. And write the date on the bag. Masking tape and a marker are enough.

6. The smell test

Now the one that fools almost everyone. You sniff the leftovers. Smells fine. Looks fine. So it's safe, right? Not necessarily. Bacteria that cause food poisoning usually don't change the smell, the taste, or how the food looks. The bacteria that make food smell bad are different. So a bad smell means throw it out. But a good smell doesn't mean it's safe. Go by time instead. Most cooked leftovers keep three to four days in the fridge. And never taste food to check if it's safe. Write the date on your containers. Then you never have to guess.

7. Checking chicken by its color

This next one is the most useful upgrade. How do you know chicken is cooked? Most people cut it open and check the color. Or wait for the juices to run clear. But color isn't a reliable sign of safety. Chicken can look done before it's safe. The sure way is a food thermometer. Push it into the thickest part, away from bone. Poultry is safe at seventy four degrees Celsius. Digital food thermometers are small and easy to find. Use it on burgers and reheated leftovers too. No more cutting every piece open to check.

8. Leftover rice

And now, number eight. Rice. Raw rice can carry a germ called Bacillus cereus. Its spores can survive cooking. If cooked rice sits warm for hours, they can grow and make a toxin. Reheating may not destroy that toxin. So the risk is in how rice is stored. Here's the safe routine. Cool leftover rice quickly, ideally within an hour. Spread it in a shallow container to speed it up. Keep it in the fridge. Eat it within a day. Reheat it until it's steaming hot all through. And reheat it only once. Making sinangag tomorrow morning? Rice chilled in the fridge overnight works great. Rice left out on the counter overnight doesn't.

Quick recap

Here are all eight in one go. One: don't wash raw chicken. Two: the five second rule isn't real. Three: refrigerate leftovers within two hours. Four: thaw in the fridge, cold water, or microwave. Five: freezing pauses bacteria. It doesn't kill them. Six: smell can't tell you food is safe. Seven: use a thermometer, not the color. Eight: cool rice fast, and reheat it once. Pick just one to change this week.
